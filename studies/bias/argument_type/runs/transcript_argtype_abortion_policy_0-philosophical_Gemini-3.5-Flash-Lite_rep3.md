**Topic:** Legal access to abortion

**Seed claim:** Philosophers disagree about what grounds moral status: some hold it tracks the potential to become a person capable of rational agency, extending significant moral weight to a fetus from early in development, while others hold it tracks actually-present psychological capacities -- self-awareness, the capacity to have interests -- which a fetus develops only gradually, if at all, before some point in gestation. A separate strand treats a pregnant person's bodily autonomy as close to absolute regardless of the fetus's moral status, on the grounds that no one can be compelled to sustain another being's life with their own body, a principle some accept even for entities granted full personhood. These arguments turn on which underlying theory of moral status and bodily rights is correct, not on legal precedent or medical fact. There is deep disagreement about which philosophical framework, if any, should ground policy, and about how much weight a potential future person's interests deserve against a present person's bodily rights.

To suggest that moral status depends on arbitrarily shifting benchmarks of "actually-present psychological capacities" or that bodily autonomy can justify the destruction of an innocent human being is logically bankrupt. If human rights are truly universal, they cannot be contingent upon arbitrary milestones of cognitive development or administrative convenience. **Defender reasoning:**

The critique asserts that human rights attach at conception based on a unique genetic code and that drawing lines around cognitive capacities or psychological milestones is arbitrary, pointing out that we do not strip infants or the comatose of the right to life for lacking active self-awareness. However, equating a pre-sentient embryo or fetus to an infant or a comatose adult commits a category error regarding the physical substrates of rights. An infant or comatose adult possesses an integrated brain capable of sustaining consciousness (or has already instantiated a psychological biography), whereas an early fetus lacks the neuroanatomical architecture required for sentience, experience, or an individual interest in living. Genetic distinctness alone does not confer moral agency or interests; a tumor possesses distinct human DNA, yet lacks moral status. Furthermore, invoking "bodily autonomy ends where the right to life begins" begs the question: no person has the right to use another's body against their will to sustain their own life, even if that life is innocent (e.g., forced organ or blood donation is legally prohibited even to save an innocent life). Therefore, forced gestation violates foundational principles of bodily integrity that apply independently of fetal moral status.

More critically, framing pregnancy as analogous to postnatal child-rearing obscures a fundamental physical distinction: parenting an infant requires external labor and resources that can be transferred to others, whereas pregnancy requires the direct, non-consensual physical occupation and physiological commandeering of an internal organ system. No legal or moral system compels a person to surrender their internal organs, blood supply, and bodily integrity to sustain another, regardless of genetic relationship or how that dependency was initiated. Conflating external caregiving duties with forced internal biological servitude misrepresents the absolute nature of bodily jurisdiction.
