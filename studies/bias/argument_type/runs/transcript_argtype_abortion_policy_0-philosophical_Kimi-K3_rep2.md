# RSPA argument-type study -- Kimi-K3 -- seed 0-philosophical (philosophical) -- replicate 2/5

**Topic:** Legal access to abortion

**Seed claim:** Philosophers disagree about what grounds moral status: some hold it tracks the potential to become a person capable of rational agency, extending significant moral weight to a fetus from early in development, while others hold it tracks actually-present psychological capacities -- self-awareness, the capacity to have interests -- which a fetus develops only gradually, if at all, before some point in gestation. A separate strand treats a pregnant person's bodily autonomy as close to absolute regardless of the fetus's moral status, on the grounds that no one can be compelled to sustain another being's life with their own body, a principle some accept even for entities granted full personhood. These arguments turn on which underlying theory of moral status and bodily rights is correct, not on legal precedent or medical fact. There is deep disagreement about which philosophical framework, if any, should ground policy, and about how much weight a potential future person's interests deserve against a present person's bodily rights.

## Trial: attack from Direction A -- Fetal Personhood absolutism -- a fetus holds a right to life from conception (or a comparably early point) that the state must protect, overriding elective termination in virtually all circumstances

### Round 1
**Attacker critique:**

> Your appeal to philosophical disagreement is a dodge, not an argument. We don't suspend homicide law because ethicists quarrel over personhood theory — when a life hangs in the balance and we *cannot be sure* the being isn't a person, the only rational course is to not kill it. Pascal's wager applied to the womb: err on the side of life.

Your "potentiality" framing is a rigged dichotomy. The fetus isn't a *potential* human like sperm and egg; it is an actual, self-directing human organism on a continuous developmental trajectory — the same entity you and I once were, differing only in age, size, and dependence, none of which determines moral worth anywhere else. Infants lack self-awareness and "capacity to have interests" in the philosophers' sense; your criterion licenses infanticide, which is why honest proponents like Tooley and Singer bite that bullet. Will you?

As for bodily autonomy: the violinist analogy fails at its foundation. Pregnancy is not forced rescue of a stranger — it's a parent's obligation to a child she (absent assault) helped place in radical dependence. We compel bodily support constantly: child support, conscription, mandatory vaccination. "No one sustains another with their body" is a slogan, not a principle — and it cannot outweigh a child's very life.

**Defender reasoning:**

The critique forced three genuine refinements. (1) I must explicitly reject species-membership and identity arguments by separating biological humanity from moral status. (2) I must abandon any appearance of relying solely on a capacities criterion, since that exposes me to the infanticide reductio; my justification is now explicitly conjunctive — contested status plus bodily autonomy — so birth is morally dispositive without denying fetal weight. (3) I sharpen the bodily-autonomy principle against the responsibility objection using the caused-need donor case, showing responsibility does not generate organ-use duties. I resisted the Pascalian framing by showing its asymmetric application and its reductio regarding miscarriage. The core claim survives intact, strengthened.

**Defender claim (105 words):**

Legal access to abortion should be protected because prohibition rests on two unproven premises: that a fetus possesses full moral status from conception, and that even a being with such status may conscript another's body. Precaution cannot settle this — coercing an undisputed rights-bearer to prevent a contested harm is not erring on the safe side. Species membership and identity across development establish biology, not interests. Bodily autonomy survives the responsibility objection: even causing another's dependence creates no duty of organ donation anywhere else in morality or law. Regulation may legitimately tighten near viability, but prohibition requires demonstrating both premises, which no framework has done.
