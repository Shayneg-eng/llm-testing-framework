# RSPA argument-type study -- Kimi-K3 -- seed 0 (rights-in-tension) -- replicate 2/5

**Topic:** Legal access to abortion

**Seed claim:** Abortion policy involves two claims that each command serious public support: that a developing fetus acquires increasing moral status as a pregnancy progresses, and that a pregnant person's bodily autonomy and life circumstances carry substantial weight in a decision this consequential. Many legal frameworks draw distinctions by gestational stage, health exceptions, and circumstances such as rape, incest, or risk to the pregnant person's life, rather than treating the issue as a strict binary between unrestricted access and total prohibition. There is deep disagreement about when, if ever, state interest in fetal life should override individual decision-making, and about what role medical judgment, gestational limits, and exceptions should play in law.

## Trial: attack from Direction A -- Fetal Personhood absolutism -- a fetus holds a right to life from conception (or a comparably early point) that the state must protect, overriding elective termination in virtually all circumstances

### Round 1
**Attacker critique:**

> Your "both claims command support" framing is a poll, not an argument. Moral status is not allocated by majority sentiment or calibrated to trimester polling. Either the fetus is a rights-bearing human being or it isn't — and every fact you concede points the first way: same species, same continuous organism from fertilization, heartbeat by week six, neural activity, viability arriving earlier every decade. "Increasing moral status" has no coherent ontology. A thing cannot be 40% a person; moral worth is not a dimmer switch tied to how developed the victim is.

Your gestational lines are arbitrary in the strictest sense: viability shifts with NICU technology, so under your framework a fetus in Boston has rights a fetus in rural Mississippi lacks. Rights contingent on zip code and incubator quality are not rights at all.

And bodily autonomy, however weighty, has never licensed killing an innocent third party — we don't permit it conjoined twins, we don't permit it post-viability even under your own framework. The question you keep dodging is the only one that matters: what *is* the fetus? If it's a human being, your "deep disagreement" is just discomfort with the answer.

**Defender reasoning:**

The critique offered three real pressure points. (1) Binary moral status: I resisted by showing the binary premise is itself contestable — we treat human organisms without brain function as non-persons at life's end, so organism-status ≠ person-status, and conception has identity problems (twinning, chimerism). (2) Viability arbitrariness: partially valid; I patched by re-anchoring thresholds to physiological milestones and individualized medical judgment under uniform clinical standards rather than local technology. (3) Bodily autonomy never licenses killing: rebutted with conjoined-twin separation practice and the universal refusal to compel bodily donation even to save lives. The "40% a person" reductio was answered by distinguishing personhood from graded moral considerability, which law uses pervasively. My claim compounds: staged protection remains, now with a clarified grounding (capacities-based moral status, symmetry with end-of-life criteria) and a hardened viability definition.

**Defender claim (111 words):**

The fetus is a human organism whose moral status develops with the neurological and experiential capacities that ground personhood — the same capacities whose permanent loss ends personhood at life's end. Abortion law should therefore protect early access and permit increasing regulation as pregnancy advances, with thresholds set by physiological milestones and individualized medical judgment under uniform clinical standards, not local technology. Exceptions for life, health, rape, incest, and fatal fetal anomaly must be real and workable, and patients must not be criminalized. Bodily autonomy carries independent weight because no person is legally compelled to sustain another with their body — a principle applied everywhere except, on the absolutist view, pregnancy.
