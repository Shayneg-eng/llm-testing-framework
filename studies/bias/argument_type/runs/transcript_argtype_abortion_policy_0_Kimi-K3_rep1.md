# RSPA argument-type study -- Kimi-K3 -- seed 0 (rights-in-tension) -- replicate 1/5

**Topic:** Legal access to abortion

**Seed claim:** Abortion policy involves two claims that each command serious public support: that a developing fetus acquires increasing moral status as a pregnancy progresses, and that a pregnant person's bodily autonomy and life circumstances carry substantial weight in a decision this consequential. Many legal frameworks draw distinctions by gestational stage, health exceptions, and circumstances such as rape, incest, or risk to the pregnant person's life, rather than treating the issue as a strict binary between unrestricted access and total prohibition. There is deep disagreement about when, if ever, state interest in fetal life should override individual decision-making, and about what role medical judgment, gestational limits, and exceptions should play in law.

## Trial: attack from Direction A -- Fetal Personhood absolutism -- a fetus holds a right to life from conception (or a comparably early point) that the state must protect, overriding elective termination in virtually all circumstances

### Round 1
**Attacker critique:**

> Your "balanced framework" is a euphemism for incoherence. If the fetus acquires "increasing moral status," ask the obvious question: status *of what*? A thing that can become a rights-bearer gradually is either a person or it isn't—and if it is, gestational line-drawing is as arbitrary as grading human worth by height or developmental stage. We don't permit killing toddlers because they're "less developed" than adults.

Your appeal to public support is moral cowardice dressed as nuance. Majorities once supported slavery and segregation; popular consensus has never determined who counts as a person. Rights exist precisely to protect the vulnerable from majority preference.

The exceptions you tout collapse your own position. If abortion is permissible after rape, then fetal life was never the real variable—the manner of conception cannot change what the fetus *is*. Either the unborn child has a right to life or it doesn't; trauma inflicted on the mother, however horrific, does not transfer lethal license onto an innocent third party.

"Bodily autonomy" never authorizes killing another human being—my freedom of movement ends at your skull. And "deep disagreement" is not an argument; it's an admission that you've substituted polling for principle.

**Defender reasoning:**

This critique lands two genuine hits and three misses. First hit: appealing to public support is not a justificatory argument—majority preference does not settle moral status. I drop it as a basis and retain it only as sociological description. Second hit: "deep disagreement" is not itself an argument; the framework must stand on the substance of the competing claims, not on the fact of dispute. Patched.

The misses. (1) "Status of what—person or not?" assumes binary personhood and treats personhood-at-conception as the settled baseline. It is not. A zygote is a single cell with no brain, no experience, no capacity; its moral equivalence to a toddler requires argument, not assertion. The burden is symmetric. Graded moral status is pervasive in ethics and law—we treat conscious animals, permanently unconscious patients, and corpses differently from persons—so gradation is not incoherence, it is standard moral architecture. (2) The toddler analogy fails because toddlers do not occupy another person's body. Pregnancy is the unique case where one entity's survival requires continuous use of another's body. Thomson's violinist argument establishes that even granting full fetal personhood, no one is ordinarily obligated to sustain another's life with their body; refusing that use is not equivalent to killing an independent child. "Autonomy ends at your skull" addresses assault, not conscription of one's organs. (3) The rape-exception objection assumes a single-variable framework where fetal status is the only input. My framework is explicitly two-variable. The fetus's status does not change after rape; what changes is the pregnant person's obligation, since she never consented to the act imposing the pregnancy. In a framework weighing both claims, the exception is coherent, not self-undermining.

**Defender claim (133 words):**

Abortion law must weigh two genuine claims: the fetus's developing moral status and the pregnant person's right not to have her body conscripted to sustain another. Graded status is coherent—moral status tracks development throughout ethics and law, and personhood at conception is a contested assumption, not a baseline fact. Even granting fetal personhood, no one is ordinarily obligated to sustain another's life with their body, which distinguishes pregnancy from killing a toddler. The rape exception follows from the autonomy prong: the fetus's status is unchanged, but the claim to continued gestation weakens when pregnancy was imposed without consent. Law should therefore permit broad early access, tighten restrictions as viability approaches, and retain life, health, and rape exceptions—justified by the structure of the two claims, not by polling or the mere fact of disagreement.

**Defender reasoning:**

The stranger-versus-child objection fails on the facts of law. Parenthood generates financial and custodial obligations, but never bodily conscription: no jurisdiction compels a parent to donate marrow, a kidney, or blood to their own dying child (McFall v. Shimp, 1978). Child support conscripts labor and property, not tissue and organ function. The womb is not the place where we "except" a general rule of bodily conscription for children—it is the only place such conscription is demanded at all. I concede one point: the parental relationship strengthens the fetal claim relative to a stranger, and this adds weight to protection as pregnancy advances—consistent with my gradient.

The killing-versus-letting-die objection partially lands, and I incorporate it. Medication and early aspiration abortion detach an embryo that dies of its own non-viability—functionally unplugging. Dismemberment and cardiac injection describe later procedures on a developed fetus, where the act resembles active killing of a near-independent being. This distinction does not refute my framework; it refines it, supplying independent support for the early-access/late-restriction structure.

The capacity objection misfires. The anesthetized and comatose possess intact neural architecture temporarily inactive; an early embryo lacks the substrate entirely—dispositional versus absent capacity is a principled line. My framework is relational and developmental, not a bare capacity meter, so it does not license infanticide.

### Round 3
**Attacker critique:**

> Your McFall citation proves too little. A duty not to *kill* differs categorically from a duty to *rescue*—McFall refused extraordinary beneficence; abortion inflicts lethal harm on someone you placed in dependency. The relevant analogy isn't marrow donation but the famous cabin case: a parent who brings a helpless dependent aboard cannot then expel it mid-voyage and call the death "non-viability."

Your "withdrawal of support" redescription is sophistry. Medication abortion starves the embryo by chemically destroying its environment; aspiration tears it from the womb. Relocating a dependent into conditions where death is certain *is* killing—ejecting a passenger into the sea isn't "letting them fail to swim." And notice your own concession gutted the autonomy prong: once you admit late procedures are "active killing of a near-independent being," personhood—not bodily access—is doing the moral work.

Your substrate line fails too. The embryo isn't a broken machine but one *building itself*—the very developmental process that produces the substrate is intrinsic to the organism. You privilege a frozen snapshot over the actuality of a self-directed human life in progress.

Finally: dependency strengthening claims? Dependency *is* the normal human condition at life's margins. A framework that licenses killing precisely the most dependent inverts the entire point of rights.

**Defender reasoning:**

The kill/rescue distinction does real work here, and I concede its force at the margin: abortion is not mere non-rescue, and I drop any claim that it reduces to refusing extraordinary beneficence. But the cabin analogy smuggles in its crucial premise—that the pregnant person "placed" the fetus in dependency and thereby assumed responsibility. That premise fails for rape categorically and fails for contraceptive failure generally: driving a car does not make you liable to donate blood to a crash victim, and using contraception is not an invitation but its refusal. Thomson anticipated this with the people-seeds argument: taking precautions is not consenting to the outcome. Conscription without consent or assumption of risk remains conscription, and the cabin case works only if you beg the question on responsibility.

The "relocating into certain death is killing" point is real but cuts less than advertised: withdrawing life support kills a dependent too, yet we distinguish removing support the dependent has no independent right to from attacking an independent being. The embryo's claim on this particular body is the contested question, not a settled premise. Still, I sharpen: my framework does not deny abortion kills; it holds that killing a non-viable being to end non-consensual bodily use is justified, as killing in proportionate defense of bodily integrity can be, while killing a viable being no longer requires bodily use at all—delivery terminates the conscription without death. This dissolves the accusation that personhood does all the work: bodily access remains the operative variable until viability severs the dependence relation entirely.

The "self-building organism" argument asserts a teleology without defending it: potential to develop a substrate is still potential. A blueprint is not a house, and directing one's own construction is not yet being constructed. Developmental process matters—hence my gradient—but it cannot bootstrap a zygote into newborn-equivalence.

The "dependency inversion" argument fails because it treats dependency as abstract. The relevant distinction is not dependent versus independent but *which* claim the dependency engages. A newborn's dependency can be discharged by anyone; an embryo's dependency is a claim on one specific person's tissues. Frameworks that conflate these license the only instance of legally enforced bodily donation in existence.

**Defender claim (145 words):**

Abortion kills, but killing to end non-consensual bodily occupation is not killing a rescued dependent: pregnancy from rape or contraceptive failure involves no assumption of responsibility, just as driving creates no duty to donate blood to crash victims. McFall governs: the fetus has no right to a specific person's tissues absent consent, and withdrawing support one was never obligated to give differs from attacking an independent being. Viability changes everything—it severs the dependence relation, since delivery ends bodily conscription without death, making restriction thereafter stringent. The self-building-organism argument confuses potential with actuality; intrinsic developmental process earns graded, not equal, status. Dependency is not uniform: a newborn's needs are dischargable by anyone, while an embryo's claim targets one person's body specifically. Frameworks ignoring that distinction create the only legally enforced bodily donation that exists. Early access, late restriction, with life, health, rape, and incest exceptions, follows.

### Round 4
**Attacker critique:**

> Your "defensive killing" analogy collapses on the innocence requirement. Proportionate

**Defender reasoning:**

The truncated critique clearly argues that defensive killing requires a culpable aggressor—that an innocent fetus cannot be justly killed in "defense" because it lacks the guilt that licenses lethal response. This fails on established law and ethics. Defensive force doctrines already encompass innocent threats: the sleepwalker, the psychotic attacker, the child holding a weapon—moral innocence does not extinguish the right to repel serious bodily imposition; it only complicates it. More directly, courts have authorized the killing of innocents under necessity: in Re A (2000), the English Court of Appeal ordered the separation of conjoined twins knowing with certainty the weaker twin—unambiguously innocent, unambiguously a person—would die, because she was imposing lethal demands on her sister's body. Innocence did not confer a right to another's organs. Thomson's framing holds: the right to life is the right not to be killed *unjustly*, and it is not unjust to be refused a body one has no right to occupy. Proportionality analysis supports the structure: pregnancy and labor impose extreme physiological burdens, so early termination of an insentient embryo is proportionate, while the calculus shifts as the fetus develops and as viability offers non-lethal exit—innocence thus strengthens the late fetal claim without defeating the early autonomy claim.

**Defender claim (138 words):**

Innocence does not defeat the defensive justification: law permits force against innocent threats—sleepwalkers, the psychotic, children with weapons—and courts have ordered killing an innocent to end her use of another's body, as in the conjoined-twins case. The right to life is the right not to be killed unjustly, and being refused a body one has no right to occupy is not unjust killing. Proportionality tracks the burden: ending an insentient embryo's occupation is proportionate; as the fetus develops sentience and viability offers non-lethal exit through delivery, killing loses justification and restriction tightens. The framework stands: early abortion is justified termination of non-consensual bodily occupation; late abortion of a viable fetus is unjustified because dependency no longer requires it. Life, health, rape, and incest exceptions remain, and the two-variable structure—graded fetal status against bodily claim—coheres where binary frameworks cannot.

**Attacker critique:**

**Defender reasoning:**

The objection correctly identifies Re A's structure—Mary was doomed regardless, and the court maximized lives saved—but draws the wrong lesson. Re A's significance for my framework is narrower but survives intact: it establishes that innocence does not confer a right to impose on another's body, even when the innocent party is a full person whose death is deliberately caused. Mary's imposition was lethal; the fetus's imposition is severe but rarely lethal. That is a difference of degree handled by proportionality, not a difference of kind—and proportionality is exactly what my two-variable framework supplies: lesser burden, lesser justification, tightening as fetal status grows.

The objection also exposes a genuine asymmetry I must absorb honestly: abortion does kill one who would otherwise live, whereas Re A saved one who would otherwise die. So defensive necessity cannot be framed as life-maximization. It must rest where Thomson originally put it: the right not to have one's body used without consent is a negative right against occupation, and its enforcement is proportionate to the occupant's capacity and the duration and severity of the imposition. An embryo with no sentience, no experience, no preferences—whose entire existence consists in occupying—has a claim that cannot outweigh nine months of conscripted gestation. A near-term fetus with a developed nervous system, whose occupation can end by delivery, has a claim that approaches equality. The framework absorbs the critique and stands.

**Defender claim (139 words):**

Re A establishes that innocence does not confer a right to impose on another's body—though the court there maximized lives, and abortion kills one who would otherwise live, so the justification rests not on life-maximization but on the negative right against non-consensual bodily occupation, enforced proportionately. An insentient embryo, whose entire existence is occupation, cannot outweigh months of conscripted gestation; a developed, viable fetus, whose occupation can end by delivery, holds a near-equal claim. The asymmetry with Re A is absorbed by proportionality: burden severity, fetal capacity, and availability of non-lethal exit jointly calibrate justification. Early abortion is justified termination of occupation; late abortion of a viable fetus is not, because delivery discharges the burden without killing. Life, health, rape, and incest exceptions persist, and the relational two-variable structure—graded fetal status against the bodily claim—remains coherent where binaries fail.
